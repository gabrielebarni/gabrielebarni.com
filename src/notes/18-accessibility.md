Consider an employee using a shared phone during a shift, or trying to understand a request while already worried about a personal issue. An interface that feels straightforward at a desk may be much harder in that situation.

## Design for the conditions of use

I would keep common tasks easy to find and provide another route to help. A chatbot asks the employee to formulate the right question before the service can begin. That can be useful, but should not become a condition for accessing essential support.

## Watch the complete task

Observe a varied group completing a task with their usual devices and assistive tools where relevant. Check keyboard use, instructions, error messages and recovery. Include people who do not normally work at a computer.

Technical accessibility and understandable communication both matter. Ask people what they think will happen next, rather than checking only whether the form was submitted.

<summary>Research behind this note</summary>

W3C's forms guidance recommends clear, properly associated labels so people can understand and operate controls, including through assistive technology. This is one component of accessibility, not a complete compliance test.
