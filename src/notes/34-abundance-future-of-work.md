During a slow Peloton ride, I listened to Elon Musk’s long-form interview with The Economist. One statement stayed with me: “Money won’t matter in 2036.”

I am less interested in defending that date than in exploring the question behind it. If AI makes more expertise widely available, and robotics expands what we can produce, what happens to an economy organised around scarce human capability? And what happens to work?

## From faster work to a different economy

Musk’s argument goes beyond employees getting more done. In his vision, AI supplies intelligence and robots bring it into the physical world. Goods and services become so plentiful that money loses much of its importance. His idea of universal high income belongs to that scenario—not to an economic outcome already secured.

That distinction matters. A system that helps us produce a report faster changes a task. A system that makes expertise and execution much cheaper could change which businesses are viable, what customers expect and how organisations create value. The opportunity is much larger than reducing the cost of the same work.

## Scarcity may move rather than disappear

I would be careful about declaring Adam Smith and David Ricardo obsolete. Their questions about production, specialisation and distribution remain useful, even if the conditions change dramatically.

Cheaper intelligence does not make electricity, materials, desirable locations or human attention unlimited. Nor does producing more tell us who owns the productive assets or who can access the output. Some constraints could ease while others become more important.

The economic models we learned deserve to be challenged. But the useful question is which assumptions no longer hold—not whether economics has stopped applying.

## Abundance does not distribute itself

This is where the discussion becomes a future-of-work issue. For many people, employment is the route from economic production to personal income. If less human labour is needed for some activities, how will those people participate in the value being created?

Lower prices could help. So could new work, wider ownership or income transfers. None follows automatically from a more capable model. Funding, access and the distribution of gains still require choices. A future promise of abundance does not pay the bills during a difficult career transition.

In a company, the same question appears on a smaller scale. If a team can deliver the same result with less effort, do we improve the service, grow the business, reduce working hours, invest in learning or remove roles? That is a leadership decision. The technology does not make it for us.

## Work is also identity, learning and belonging

Even if income became less dependent on employment, work would still mean more than a salary. It provides structure, relationships, recognition and a place to contribute. More freedom could be enormously positive. Losing a valued role without a credible next step is a different experience.

For people entering the workforce, the immediate issue is practical: how do you build expertise when AI can perform some of the tasks through which you would have learned? Use the technology extensively to practise, explore and test ideas. But keep opportunities to explain your reasoning, receive feedback and take real responsibility. A convincing output is not yet professional judgement.

## What I would put on the leadership agenda

We do not need to agree with the 2036 prediction to prepare. HR, finance and operations should work through a few plausible scenarios together: where capability becomes cheaper, where adoption remains difficult, and what each scenario means for roles, skills and capacity.

- Look at tasks and bottlenecks before deciding which jobs disappear.
- Agree how productivity gains will be used, including the investment needed to move people into new work.
- Protect entry routes and supervised learning while redesigning delivery.
- Explain what is changing, what remains uncertain and when decisions will be reviewed.

What stayed with me after the ride was not the idea of a world without money. It was the possibility that human labour might become less of a constraint on what we can achieve.

That could give us more choice about how we work and live. Turning that possibility into something widely beneficial will depend on the organisations and institutions we build around it.
