Consider an internal transfer. The manager wants a confirmed date, the employee wants to understand the changes, and several teams need to update their part of the process. Each team may complete its task on time. The move can still be late.

That is the problem I would put at the centre of the next HR operating-model discussion. Not because structure is unimportant, but because the business needs the whole service to work. Adding AI to individual steps will not resolve a gap in accountability between them.

Carlos Larracilla’s LinkedIn post raises a useful question: will AI make HR leaner, or create capacity for a broader contribution? My view is that we first need to be more specific about the services we intend to deliver. Different work will need different arrangements.

## The research points to choices, rather than one destination

The published perspectives do not describe one agreed future. They help frame decisions about capability, automation and investment.

### McKinsey: a different capability mix

McKinsey describes a leaner, more flexible function with stronger strategic, scientific and technological capabilities. More sophisticated expertise does not necessarily mean a larger HR organisation.

Source: [McKinsey · A new operating model for people management: More personal, more tech, more human](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/people-and-organization/our-insights/a-new-operating-model-for-people-management-more-personal-more-tech-more-human/) (Primary article)

### Bain: change the roles as well as the tasks

Bain connects automation and augmentation with reallocating time and redefining responsibilities across HR. Its time-saving estimates are not a guaranteed outcome for a particular organisation.

Source: [Bain · Better, Faster, Leaner: Reinventing HR with Generative AI](https://www.bain.com/insights/better-faster-leaner-reinventing-hr-with-generative-ai/) (19 November 2024)

### Gartner: move beyond isolated pilots

Gartner’s public abstract calls for operating-model transformation. It does not, on its own, establish a particular headcount direction; this note does not rely on the subscriber research.

Source: [Gartner · Preparing the Transformation to an AI-Infused HR Operating Model](https://www.gartner.com/en/documents/7425462) (10 February 2026 · public abstract only)

The business decision remains ours: which service needs to improve, which capabilities we need to retain or build, and what we intend to do with any released capacity. These questions need alignment before a productivity estimate becomes a workforce commitment.

## Design around the kind of help people need

I would distinguish four service patterns. They can sit within the same function, and one employee journey may use all four.

### Clear information

Make approved guidance easy to find and apply. The answer needs the right context, a source that is maintained and a route to help when the situation is unclear.

### Repeatable transactions

Use controlled workflows for well-defined requests. Specify the permitted changes, approval points and how an error is corrected. Confirm the outcome to the person who requested it.

### Cases requiring judgement

Give sensitive or unusual situations a named professional with the right expertise. AI may support preparation, but the employee needs access to someone who can understand the circumstances and take responsibility.

### Strategic business work

Bring HRBPs, specialists and business leaders together around a defined organisational question. Use technology to support analysis and explore options. Keep the decision and its trade-offs with the accountable leader.

The practical test is the handoff. Can the service recognise that a request needs a different level of support, and move it without asking the employee to start again?

## Give the transfer one owner, even if several teams deliver it

Return to the internal move. An assistant might explain the process. A workflow could collect confirmed details and obtain approvals. Payroll and IT would complete their respective changes, while a specialist handles any exception. This is an illustrative design, not a report of a deployed system.

Who confirms that the move can take place? That is the accountability to settle. The owner needs visibility of dependencies and an agreed route to resolve conflicts between teams. A coordinator who can only send reminders will struggle when priorities compete.

The technology behind the service needs ownership too. Approved knowledge, data quality, access controls, testing and incident response require capacity after launch. I would include that work in the operating model and the budget from the beginning.

## Agree what the capacity is for

If the service becomes more efficient, the next discussion is about how to use the benefit. It may mean a shorter backlog, more access to specialists, better workforce planning or lower cost. Those are different commitments.

Small amounts of time released across many people do not automatically become a removable position or a new strategic capability. Work has to be reorganised, priorities changed and, often, skills developed. The business case needs to show how that happens.

Include time transferred to employees, managers and other functions. Otherwise, HR can report an improvement while the organisation experiences more work elsewhere.

## Start with one journey and a decision we can make

A 90-day pilot can be a useful planning frame, provided the scope is realistic and the timetable does not override unresolved risk.

### First month: understand the service

Map the journey with users and delivery teams. Agree the owner and baseline, repair the source knowledge and define the limits of automation.

### Second month: test and learn

Use synthetic cases before a bounded live pilot. Test exceptions and escalation. Track review effort, errors and corrections alongside speed.

### Third month: make the investment decision

Compare complete outcomes with the baseline and, where feasible, a comparable group. Include maintenance and oversight. Decide whether to extend the service, change it or stop.

I would review completion quality, elapsed time, repeat contacts, employee effort and total cost together. Then read a few difficult cases with the numbers. They often explain where the process is still creating work.

## The question for the leadership team

We do not need to agree today whether HR everywhere will be larger or smaller in 2030. We do need to agree what our business requires from HR, who owns the delivery and how we will know it has improved.

Start there. The structure and technology choices will be more useful once the service commitment is clear.
