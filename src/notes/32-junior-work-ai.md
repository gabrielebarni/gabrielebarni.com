Imagine arriving at an interview with a dashboard, a research brief and a working prototype. AI helped you prepare all three. The interviewer changes one assumption and asks you to explain what happens next. Can you still make sense of the work?

That is the opportunity, and the challenge, for people entering the world of work. I would encourage them to use the technology seriously. Explore, build, ask better questions and attempt things that were previously out of reach. But be clear about what you understand and what you still need to learn.

For employers, there is a corresponding responsibility. We cannot raise the expectations of junior roles and quietly remove the support that helps people meet them.

## The skills signal needs a careful reading

McKinsey’s HR Monitor exhibit shows increases in top-five future-skill mentions for digital literacy, problem-solving and creativity: 15, 9 and 6 percentage points respectively between 2025 and 2026. The comparisons are directional. Country coverage changed, with 1,303 respondents in 2026 and 1,925 in 2025.

These are reported priorities, not vacancy counts or hiring results. My practical reading is that tool fluency needs to sit alongside the ability to frame a problem, explore alternatives and explain a decision.

## Finishing the task and learning the task are different outcomes

A completed output can hide an important gap. Does the person know why a calculation works, which assumptions matter and what would make the answer unreliable? That gap matters when the next assignment arrives.

Shen and Tamkin’s experiments with developers learning an unfamiliar coding library found poorer learning outcomes with AI assistance and no significant average efficiency gain. Some cognitively engaged usage patterns preserved learning. This is a bounded coding study, not a verdict on every tool or profession.

Source: [Shen & Tamkin · How AI Impacts Skill Formation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20245v2) (Research preprint · revised 1 February 2026; coding experiment, not an all-occupations result)

For a learner, I would keep a simple record beside the work: what I can explain, what I checked and what still needs review. The point is to make progress visible, rather than conceal the uncertainty behind a polished result.

## Use AI for more than the application letter

There are practical ways to make the tools part of a serious preparation effort. These are suggestions for practice, not a formula for getting hired.

### Understand the work

Choose a role and build a simple map of its tasks, language and stakeholders. Use AI to surface questions, then verify important answers through documentation and conversations with practitioners.

### Build something small

Create a narrow prototype with public or synthetic data. A data-quality check or simple dashboard is enough if you can explain the choices and maintain it.

### Find the weaknesses

Ask for counterexamples and then test them. Agreement from another model is not a substitute for checking a source, reconciling a total or running a reproducible test.

### Explain it to a person

Practise describing the result, its limits and the next decision. A manager needs to understand what the work supports, not just see an impressive demonstration.

Personal practice should use non-sensitive material. At work, follow the organisation’s approved tools and data rules. Curiosity is valuable; access to a tool does not authorise access to every input.

## A modest portfolio can support a much better conversation

Take a hypothetical junior HR analyst building an onboarding dashboard. Use synthetic records and start with a practical question: where is preparation for the start date getting delayed? AI can help draft the calculations. The analyst still needs to define the measures.

Introduce duplicate entries, missing dates and a department with only two cases. Is the dashboard counting people or tasks? Does a blank date mean late, or simply unknown? Does that small sample support the recommendation?

Keep those questions and corrections with the final result. Explain what AI contributed and where a practitioner’s review changed the approach. Label the project as simulated; do not imply an employer used it or that it delivered savings.

Now the interview can explore the person’s reasoning and development. That is a stronger proposition than presenting an elaborate project they cannot explain.

## Look for a role with a real development commitment

A candidate can bring preparation and initiative. They should also ask what the employer will provide. Which tools are approved? Who reviews a new starter’s work? What should the person be able to handle independently after three months?

I would listen carefully to the answers. A team that can describe the support and progression is offering something more concrete than a promise of exposure to AI. Candidates do not all have the same money, time or networks; selection should not mistake those advantages for potential.

For HR leaders, the role specification should set out both the contribution expected and the learning the organisation will support. Review time needs to be planned. Independence should be earned against sound criteria, not measured by agreement with an AI answer that may itself be wrong.

## Make the first month manageable

For a student, career changer or new starter, I would suggest one small learning project rather than several unfinished demonstrations.

### Understand

Choose the problem, read the relevant guidance and define a result you can test. Write down the questions you cannot yet answer.

### Build and check

Create the prototype with safe data. Test awkward inputs, keep a contribution log and fix a meaningful weakness before adding features.

### Get feedback

Ask a tutor, peer or practitioner to review the uncertain parts. Record what changed and which points remain open.

### Try a variation

Change the conditions and use less assistance. Explain your decisions. Update the portfolio with what you learned, rather than a claim to have mastered the profession.

Adjust this to the time and access available. It is a development exercise, not an expectation that candidates do unpaid production work.

## The ambition is to become useful, and keep growing

AI can help a newcomer contribute sooner. It cannot provide every relationship, every piece of business context or the experience of taking responsibility when the result is wrong.

Use the tools with energy. Bring evidence, questions and the willingness to be corrected. Employers need to meet that effort with opportunities to learn the job properly.
