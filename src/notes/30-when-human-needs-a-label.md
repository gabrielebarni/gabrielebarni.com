*Cambridge Dictionary picked 'human' as its word of the year, and what interests me is that we now need to say it at all.*

## A word we never had to say

This week Cambridge Dictionary [named 'human' its Word of the Year 2026](https://www.cambridge.org/news-and-insights/human-is-Cambridge-dictionary-word-of-the-year-2026). I smiled when I read it, because it's such an ordinary word to win anything. Their lexicographer, Colin McIntosh, puts it this way: "AI is changing how we talk about being human." He points to "a recent increase in compounds such as 'human-made'", and says the word "has come to distinguish works or qualities of people, as distinct from AI."

'Agent' and 'analogue' were on the shortlist, and the release mentions the Authors Guild's 'Human Authored' mark for books.

## Drawing the line

There's an old idea in sociology that helps me here. Niklas Luhmann took it from the mathematician George Spencer-Brown, whose *Laws of Form* starts with a simple instruction: draw a distinction. Every observation splits the world in two: the marked side we point at, and an unmarked side that stays in the background as the default nobody mentions.

For most of history, 'human' was that default. If you got a letter or a performance review, a person had written it, and nobody thought to say so. Now that machines can write text and propose decisions that look a lot like ours, the default isn't safe anymore, and we've started marking it with words like 'human-made' and 'human in the loop'.

We've seen this before. Nobody labelled a chair 'handmade' until factories made machine-made the default. Industrialisation moved craft onto the marked side, and markets today are full of that label. What's different now is what gets marked. Back then it was objects. Now it's text, judgement and decisions, the work we assumed was human by definition.

## What the label promises

This is where it gets practical for organisations, and for HR in particular. When we tell employees or customers that a person decided or checked something, we're making a promise about where the judgement sat and who stands behind it.

"Reviewed by a human" doesn't tell anyone much if nobody can say who did the review and whether they could actually have said no. Without a named owner, the label is mostly decoration.

If we [onboard AI agents like employees](/writing/agents-onboarded-like-employees/), we also have to be clear about which steps stay with people. And companies that [run their own open models](/writing/open-weights-new-linux/) get to draw those lines themselves rather than inherit a vendor's.

## Who owns the decision

So for me the real question is who decides what 'human' means in your organisation. Which decisions need a named person behind them, and who answers when the label turns out to be wrong?

That can't be left to a tool setting. It's an accountability policy, and it should sit with leaders who are willing to be held to it.

Cambridge picked 'human' because it stopped being obvious. Inside a company, the word only means something if a real name sits next to it.
