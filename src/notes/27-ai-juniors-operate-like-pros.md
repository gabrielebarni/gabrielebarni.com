Most of the conversation about AI and early careers is about loss. Which entry-level jobs disappear first, and how fast. That question is real and I'll come back to it. But I think it hides a bigger one: what happens when someone at the start of their career has access to expertise that used to take years, and a lot of luck, to reach?

## The junior with three advisors

Think about how most of us learned. You shadowed someone senior and hoped they had time to teach. If you were lucky, they did. You watched how they handled a difficult conversation, how they read a contract, how they decided what mattered. If you were less lucky, they were busy, impatient, or simply not there. You learned anyway, slowly, by trial and error.

Now picture a junior with three AI agents beside them all day. One advises: here's how people usually approach this, here's what to read first. One challenges: what's your assumption, what would make this wrong, what did you skip? One teaches: explain it back to me, now try it without help. None of them sighs. None of them is in a meeting. The "trivial, naive question" that nobody wanted to ask in front of the team gets asked at 11 pm, three times in a row, until it makes sense.

That's not a small change. Access to a patient expert used to depend on who you sat next to. It can now be available to everyone who shows up curious.

## What changes for seniors

Seniors don't become less valuable here. Their value moves.

Years of experience show up mostly as judgment: knowing which problem is the real one, which risk is worth taking, which person to call. A lot of senior time today still goes on work that sits below that judgment, like drafting, compiling, chasing, and re-explaining the same context. AI can take much of that, and it can extend a senior person's reach. Their way of framing a problem can be written down, shared, and used by people they've never met.

The best seniors I've worked with were generous with what they knew. AI gives that generosity scale. The catch is that it only works if they stay close to the people they're developing, not just to the tools.

## The honest risks

I'm optimistic, not naive. Three things worry me.

**Over-reliance.** An agent that always has an answer makes it easy to stop forming your own. A junior can produce senior-looking work without understanding why it works, and the gap only shows when something unusual happens. That's usually the moment it matters most.

**Losing the struggle.** Some of the friction in early careers is waste. Some of it is exactly how judgment forms: getting it wrong, being corrected, and owning the result. If AI removes all the difficulty, it can also remove the learning. A good advisor doesn't hand you the answer every time, and we should expect the same from the tools.

**Cutting the ladder.** This is the big one. A company can look at AI and conclude it doesn't need juniors at all. It may even save money for a while. But seniors don't appear from nowhere. Stop hiring and growing people at the start and in ten years you'll have no one ready for the roles that need judgment, the ones AI can't own. That isn't an efficiency gain. It's borrowing from the future.

## What HR and leaders should do differently

This is where intent becomes design. A few things I'd change.

**Redesign entry-level roles, don't delete them.** Separate the routine work that should go from the work where judgment forms. Then give juniors bigger, more real problems sooner, with AI as support and a clear owner for the outcome.

**Treat AI as part of mentoring, not a replacement for it.** The agents can answer the hundred small questions. Human mentors should spend their time on what agents can't do well: context, relationships, standards, and honest feedback on how someone thinks. That's a better use of a senior's hour than proofreading.

**Assess understanding, not output.** If AI helps everyone produce a polished first draft, the draft stops telling you much. Ask people to explain their reasoning, change one assumption and see what they do, or handle a variation with less help. Make it normal to show what the AI contributed and what they checked themselves.

**Make "naive questions" a strength.** The point of a patient advisor is that people ask more. Leaders should reward curiosity, including in front of the team, so the learning doesn't happen only in private with a tool.

**Hold the line on hiring juniors.** Be explicit that developing early-career talent is a business decision about future capability, not a cost to cut when budgets tighten.

## Where this leaves us

Juniors will still exist. If we design it well, their work can become more meaningful, more challenging, and more exciting than it has ever been, because the dull parts shrink and the interesting parts arrive sooner. If we design it badly, we'll get a generation that looks productive and can't yet decide anything, or no generation at all.

The technology doesn't choose between those outcomes. Leaders do.
